The model. Here is the whole thing, as one formula. Don't try to read it yet; by the end of the page every symbol will make sense. In plain words, it says: turn the last few words into numbers, let the last word look back at the others for clues, give each of the eight possible next words a score, and turn those scores into probabilities.

\[ P(\,\text{next word}\mid x_1,\dots,x_n) \;=\; \smO\!\Big( \big( \xn + \smA\!\big( \xn \WQ\,(\Xm \WK)^{\T} / \rt \big)\, \Xm \WV \big)\, \WO \Big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} & P(\,\text{next word}\mid x_1,\dots,x_n) = \\ & \smO\!\Big( \big( \xn \\ & \quad + \smA\!\big( \xn \WQ\,(\Xm \WK)^{\T} / \rt \big) \\ & \quad\;\; \Xm \WV \big)\, \WO \Big) \end{aligned} \]

The model's 44 numbers. These are the model's parameters: the only thing that makes it this particular model rather than another one with the same formula. Nobody chose them by hand. They were learned from example text (chapter 11). You don't need to know what each one does yet.

This page teaches you to read that formula from scratch, starting from nothing more than arithmetic. Then we'll ask a harder question: what if, for the giant models behind real AI systems, a formula like this is the best explanation there is?

1

Fitting a model to data

Long before neural networks, statisticians spoke of fitting a model to data. It's the same idea that produces a language model, so it's where we start.

Suppose a survey records five people's years of education and their yearly income, in thousands of dollars:

Years of education, \(x\)Income, \(y\)
1028
1234
1441
1649
1854

You might guess that income rises roughly in a straight line with education:

\[ y = a + b\,x \]

Here \(x\) and \(y\) stand for the two measurements, and \(a\) and \(b\) are numbers we haven't chosen yet. \(a\) is where the line starts: its height at \(x = 0\), the far left of the chart below. \(b\) is how much it climbs for each extra year, so changing \(b\) swings the line around that starting point. Each choice of \(a\) and \(b\) gives a different line, so this formula isn't one model. It's a whole family of them: every straight line at once.

Fitting means using the data to pick one member of the family. Try it: move the sliders and watch the squares. Each square's side is the gap between a person's real income and the line's prediction, so its area is that gap squared.

The standard choice, called least squares, picks the line that makes the total area of the squares as small as possible. Squaring does two jobs: misses above and below the line both count as positive, and big misses count much more than small ones. In symbols:

\[ \text{error}(a, b) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{5} \big(\, y_i - (a + b\,x_i) \,\big)^2 \]

Read it like this: for each person \(i\) from 1 to 5, take their real income \(y_i\), subtract the line's prediction \(a + b\,x_i\), square the result, and the big \(\Sigma\) says add all five up. The best line is \(\hat{y} = -5.7 + 3.35\,x\). The little hat on \(\hat y\) marks it as a prediction rather than a measurement.

Notice that the best line starts below zero: taken literally, it says someone with no schooling earns −$5,700 a year. Nobody in the data had fewer than 10 years of school, so the line was never fitted anywhere near \(x = 0\). A fitted model is only trustworthy close to the data it was fitted to, a point that comes back in chapter 14.

Keep three things apart, because the whole page depends on the difference:

  • The data: the five real observations.
  • The model family: \(y = a + b\,x\), with \(a\) and \(b\) left open.
  • The fitted model: \(\hat y = -5.7 + 3.35\,x\), the one line the data chose.

The language model at the top of this page is exactly this kind of thing: a family of formulas with 44 open slots instead of two, and data that chose the 44 numbers. The fitted line has one more property worth noticing now. Its numbers mean something you can say in a sentence: each extra year of school goes with about $3,350 more income a year. Hold on to the question of whether the language model's numbers do too.

How to read the symbols

Mathematical notation is a compressed way of writing instructions. You don't need much of it, and each piece is introduced again when it first matters. Here is the whole vocabulary in one place.

\(x,\ a,\ b\)A letter standing in for a number that isn't fixed yet, or that changes from case to case.
\(x_3\)A small number below is a label, not math: "\(x\) number 3". If \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) are the words of a phrase, \(x_3\) is the third word.
\(x_n\)The last one, whichever that is. \(n\) is the count.
\((0.8,\ -2.3)\)A vector: a list of numbers in a fixed order. Ours have two numbers each, so each one is also a point on a flat plane.
\(E,\ W_Q,\ X\)Capital letters are matrices: tables of numbers. \(E\) has one row per word and two columns.
\(x\,W\)Writing things side by side means multiply them. For matrices that has a special meaning, explained in chapter 4.
\(\textstyle\sum_{j}\)"Add up, over every \(j\)."
\(e^{z}\)The number \(e \approx 2.718\) raised to the power \(z\). Always positive, and it grows very fast as \(z\) grows. Chapter 6 says more about what \(e\) is.
\(\sqrt{2}\)The square root of 2, about 1.414.
\(M^{\top}\)"Transpose": flip a table so its rows become columns.
\(P(A \mid B)\)The probability of \(A\), given that \(B\) is true. Read the bar as "given".
\(f(x)\)A function: a rule that takes \(x\) in and gives something back. The whole model is one function.
\(\log P\)The logarithm: the power you'd have to raise \(e\) to in order to get \(P\). It undoes \(e^{z}\). For probabilities it's always zero or negative: \(\log 1 = 0\), \(\log 0.5 \approx -0.69\).
\(\theta\)The Greek letter theta, used for all of a model's adjustable numbers at once.

Throughout the page, colored symbols in equations can be hovered (or tapped) to see what they stand for. The equation pinned to the top of the screen fills in with color as you learn each part.

Words become numbers

A computer can't multiply the word "cat". So the first thing the model does is swap each word for a fixed list of numbers. Our model's whole language has eight words:

The period counts as a word too. It's how the model ends a sentence.

Each word gets two numbers. Two numbers are exactly what you need to mark a point on a flat plane (across, then up), so every word in the language is a point. These lists are called the words' embeddings.

We didn't choose these numbers. Training did (chapter 11 shows how). But look at where it put things. chases and sees sit on exactly the same point, \((3.7,\ 2.1)\). Nobody told the model that these two verbs are alike. In the text it learned from, both are always followed by "the" and another animal, so training pushed them to almost the same spot: within a few hundredths of each other, and identical once rounded to one decimal place. cat and dog are close together too, far from the verbs. The numbers aren't arbitrary; they already record something about how words behave.

Stacked as a table, one row per word, the embeddings form the matrix \(\Ev\):

A phrase becomes a table

To feed the model a phrase, look up each word's row and stack them. The phrase "the cat sleeps" becomes a 3-row table we'll call \(\Xm\). Its rows are \(x_1, x_2, x_3\), and the last row, \(\xn\), belongs to the word the model is about to follow.

The model only ever reads the last three words. Older words simply fall out of view. That limit is the model's context window. Real language models have them too, just thousands of words long.

4

Multiplying by a matrix

The model's basic move is to take a word's two numbers and turn them into two new numbers by multiplying by a 2×2 matrix. Here's the rule, in full, writing the word's two numbers as \(u_1\) and \(u_2\):

\[ (u_1,\ u_2)\begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \;=\; (\,u_1 a + u_2 c,\;\; u_1 b + u_2 d\,) \]

Each new number is a weighted blend of the old ones. The first column \((a, c)\) gives the recipe for the first new number: \(a\) parts of \(u_1\) plus \(c\) parts of \(u_2\). The second column is the recipe for the second. That's all matrix multiplication is: going down each column, multiplying pairs and adding.

With real numbers: the model has a matrix called \(\WQ\) (chapter 7 explains its job). Multiply cat's numbers by it:

\[ (0.8,\ -2.3)\begin{pmatrix} -2.1 & 0.5\\ -1.5 & 1.4 \end{pmatrix} = \big(\,0.8\times(-2.1) + (-2.3)\times(-1.5),\;\; 0.8\times 0.5 + (-2.3)\times 1.4\,\big) = (1.77,\ -2.82) \]

Step through it yourself with any word and any of the model's three 2×2 matrices:

What a matrix does to the whole plane

Since every word is a point, multiplying all of them by the same matrix moves every point at once. A matrix can stretch the plane, squash it, rotate it or flip it, but it always keeps straight lines straight and keeps the center fixed. Watch the grid and the eight words move:

Two more facts and you have all the matrix multiplication the model uses:

  • To multiply a whole table \(\Xm\) by a matrix, multiply each row separately. Three rows in, three rows out.

    Take "the cat sleeps" from chapter 3. Its table \(\Xm\) has one row per word. Multiply it by the model's matrix \(\WK\) (the formula really does this, in chapter 7):

    \[ \begin{aligned} &\begin{matrix}\text{the}\\ \text{cat}\\ \text{sleeps}\end{matrix}\; \htmlClass{c-word}{\begin{pmatrix} -3.8 & 1.5\\ 0.8 & -2.3\\ -3.0 & 5.1 \end{pmatrix}} \htmlClass{c-key}{\begin{pmatrix} 2.3 & -1.4\\ -1.8 & 0.0 \end{pmatrix}} \\[6pt] &= \begin{pmatrix} -11.44 & 5.32\\ 5.98 & -1.12\\ -16.08 & 4.20 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    Each row of the answer is one row of \(\Xm\) multiplied on its own, the same way as cat's numbers above. For the cat row, going down \(\WK\)'s two columns:

    \[ \begin{aligned} 0.8\times 2.3 + (-2.3)\times(-1.8) &= 5.98\\ 0.8\times(-1.4) + (-2.3)\times 0.0 &= -1.12 \end{aligned} \]

    That gives \((5.98,\ -1.12)\), the middle row of the answer. The rows for "the" and "sleeps" are worked out the same way, each using only its own two numbers. The rows never mix, so three rows go in and three come out, one per word.

  • The matrix doesn't have to be square. A vector of 2 numbers times a 2×8 matrix gives 8 numbers, one per column. The model uses exactly this at the end to give every word in the language a score.

    Take any two numbers, say \((1,\ 2)\), and multiply them by the model's 2×8 matrix \(\WO,\) which has one column per word:

    \[ (1,\ 2)\;\htmlClass{c-out}{\begin{pmatrix} 6.1 & -3.7 & -4.2 & -0.9 & 0.4 & -1.4 & -0.2 & 1.1\\ -2.5 & 3.0 & 0.1 & -4.1 & -4.7 & -3.9 & -4.4 & 3.9 \end{pmatrix}} \]

    Each column gives one number, worked out the same way as before. The first column belongs to "the", the second to "cat", and so on:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{the:}&\quad 1\times 6.1 + 2\times(-2.5) = 1.1\\ \text{cat:}&\quad 1\times(-3.7) + 2\times 3.0 = 2.3\\ \text{dog:}&\quad 1\times(-4.2) + 2\times 0.1 = -4.0 \end{aligned} \]

    and so on across all eight columns. Two numbers go in and eight come out, one for each word:

    \[ \begin{array}{cccc} \text{the} & \text{cat} & \text{dog} & \text{runs}\\ 1.1 & 2.3 & -4.0 & -9.1\\[10pt] \text{sleeps} & \text{chases} & \text{sees} & \text{.}\\ -9.0 & -9.2 & -9.0 & 8.9 \end{array} \]

    In chapter 8 the two numbers going in will be the model's summary of the phrase so far, and the eight coming out will be the scores for each possible next word.

5

The dot product: how much two arrows agree

Think of a vector as an arrow from the center of the plane to its point. The dot product of two arrows multiplies their matching numbers and adds:

\[ a \cdot b \;=\; a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 \]

The result measures agreement, scaled by size. It's large and positive when the arrows point the same way, near zero when they're at right angles, and negative when they point apart. Longer arrows push the number further from zero, whichever sign it has. In fact the dot product always equals

\[ a \cdot b \;=\; (\text{length of } a) \times (\text{length of } b) \times (\text{alignment}) \]

where the alignment is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 at right angles and −1 when they point in opposite directions. (Mathematicians call it the cosine of the angle between them.) So it's the alignment that always stays between −1 and 1; the dot product itself is the alignment scaled up by both lengths, and can be any size. Two long arrows pointing the same way give a big number, not 1. Only when both arrows have length 1 are the dot product and the alignment the same, which the switch below lets you try. Both lengths count as much as the alignment does, and that will matter in chapter 8. Drag the arrow tips:

The dot product is how the model asks two questions. In chapter 7: how relevant is that earlier word to this one? In chapter 8: how well does each possible next word fit? (If you noticed that matrix multiplication was also "multiply pairs and add": yes, each new number in chapter 4 was a dot product of the vector with one column.)

6

Softmax: turning scores into probabilities

The model produces scores, and scores can be any numbers at all: \(-64\), \(3\), \(25\). Probabilities have to be between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. Softmax converts one into the other in two steps:

  1. Raise \(e\) to the power of each score. Every result is positive, the order is kept, and gaps get exaggerated: a score 1 higher becomes about 2.7 times bigger.
  2. Divide each result by their total, so they add up to 1.
\[ \sm(z)_i \;=\; \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \]

Read it as: the share for item \(i\) is \(e^{z_i}\) divided by the sum of \(e^{z_j}\) over every item \(j\).

What is \(e\)? It's a particular number, about 2.71828, that turns up all over mathematics the way \(\pi\) does. One way to meet it: if something grows continuously at a rate of 100% per unit of time, compounding every instant, then after one unit of time it has multiplied by exactly \(e\), not by 2. You can watch it appear by computing \((1 + \tfrac{1}{n})^{n}\) for bigger and bigger \(n\): 2, then 2.25, 2.37, 2.44, and so on, creeping up toward 2.71828. Softmax could use any number bigger than 1 in its place; using 2 instead would be the same as multiplying every score by about 0.69. It uses \(e\) because \(e^{z}\) has a special property: at every point, its slope is equal to its own value. That makes the calculus of training unusually tidy, and it's part of why the error signal in chapter 12 comes out as simply "predicted minus actual".

Two things to notice by playing with it. Adding the same amount to every score changes nothing, because only the gaps matter. And when one score is ahead by more than about 5, it takes nearly all of the probability. The model's scores are often far apart, which is why many of its predictions come out at 99.9% or more (shown as 100%).

7

Attention: letting a word look back

Here is the problem attention solves. After "cat chases the" the next word should be dog. After "dog chases the" it should be cat. In both cases the last word is "the", so the last word alone can't decide. The model has to look back and see who is doing the chasing.

Attention lets the last word pull in information from the words before it. It takes four moves, each built from the tools in chapters 4 to 6.

  1. Give every word three new vectors by multiplying its embedding by three matrices: a query \(\qv = x\,\WQ\), what this word is looking for; a key \(\kv = x\,\WK\), what a word offers to be found by; and a value \(\vv = x\,\WV\), what a word hands over if it's chosen. Only the last word needs a query, because only it is making the prediction.
  2. Score each word with the dot product of the last word's query and that word's key: \(s_j = \qv \cdot \kv_j\). Then divide by \(\rt\). (Real models divide by the square root of how many numbers each vector holds, so that scores don't grow just because vectors hold more numbers. Ours hold 2, so here it's a small, fixed shrink.)
  3. Softmax the scores into attention weights \(\av_j\) that add up to 1.
  4. Blend the values using those weights: \(\zv = \sum_j \av_j\, \vv_j\). A word with weight 1 hands over its whole value; a word with weight 0 contributes nothing.

The names query, key and value are metaphors. The matrices are just numbers training found. But the metaphor fits what they end up doing, as you can check below with every number shown.

With "cat chases the", about 99.6% of the attention lands on cat, so the blend is almost exactly cat's value. The last word has found the subject. Switch to "chases the cat" and the word cat looks back at chases instead: that's how it knows it's the object and the sentence should end. With ". the cat" it looks at the period and knows a verb comes next.

Now try "dog chases the". The attention splits between dog and chases, yet the result still leads to cat. Training found a solution that works, not the tidy one a person might have designed. That gap between "works" and "tidy" comes back in chapter 14.

All of attention as one expression

Write the keys of all the words as rows of a table, \(\Xm\WK\). Flipping it with \({}^{\T}\) turns the keys into columns, so multiplying the query by it computes every score at once (each column gives one dot product). That gives the whole of attention in one line:

\[ \zv \;=\; \smA\!\Big(\, \xn \WQ\, (\Xm\WK)^{\T} / \rt \,\Big)\; \Xm \WV \]

Inside the brackets: query times keys, scaled, are the scores. Softmax turns them into weights. Multiplying the weights by the table of values \(\Xm\WV\) is the blend.

8

Making the prediction

Attention has produced \(\zv\), two numbers summarizing what the last word found by looking back. Three short steps turn that into a prediction.

  1. Add the word back in. \(r = \xn + \zv\). Attention's findings are added on top of what the last word already was, so neither is lost. Real models do this everywhere; it's called a residual connection.
  2. Score every word in the language. Multiply \(r\) by the 2×8 matrix \(\WO\). Each of its eight columns is an arrow belonging to one word, and each score is the dot product of \(r\) with that word's arrow. As in chapter 5, a word scores high when its arrow points the same way as \(r\) and when its arrow is long. Near the top, small differences in direction barely matter, so a longer arrow can beat one that is better aimed.
  3. Softmax the eight scores into eight probabilities.

For "cat chases the", \(r\) is long and points closer to dog's arrow than to any other, so dog's score beats the rest by a wide margin and its probability comes out above 99.9%. Switch phrases and watch \(r\) swing toward a different word's arrow. After "the cat", \(r\) lands in a region where the four verbs' arrows all score about equally, which is why the model spreads its bets across them.

One honest caveat: training produced numbers with many decimal places, and we rounded each to one decimal so the equation is readable. That changes the model slightly. Before rounding it reproduced Tinyspeak's habits almost exactly (cats sleep 36% of the time, against 35% in the text); after rounding, cats sleep 42%, and "the" is followed by dog 58% of the time instead of 50%. The grammar survives rounding: in a test of about 1,550 sampled sentences, both versions broke a rule only once or twice. The rounded equation is the one used everywhere on this page.

Without the shorthand

Matrices are an abbreviation. Unpack every one of them into plain multiplications and additions and you get a formula that uses nothing but numbers, \(+\), \(\times\), \(\div\) and exp( ). That's just another way of writing \(e\) raised to a power: \(\exp(z)\) means \(e^{z}\), the same \(e\) from softmax in chapter 6. It's written this way because the powers here are hundreds of characters long, far too long to set as small raised text. Every exp in the formula comes from one of the two softmaxes: the one that makes the attention weights and the one that makes the final probabilities. For a three-word input, the probability of a single next word is one line about 12,000 characters long. Below it is written out with your phrase's numbers plugged in. Switch to symbols to see the version that works for any phrase, where \(x_{11}\) means "the first number of the first word", \(x_{12}\) "the second number of the first word", and so on.

It's long because nothing is reused. Each attention weight is spelled out again everywhere it's needed, and the denominator of the final softmax repeats the whole calculation for all eight words. That is what "one equation" literally means. Keep its length in mind for chapter 13.

Run it yourself

Type with the word tiles, or let the model write. Every number below is computed live from the equation in chapter 9. Open "Show the calculation" to see each step, and "Edit the 44 numbers" to change the model and see what breaks.

Some things worth trying:

  • Click Add the likeliest word a few times from "the". Always picking the top word makes the model repeat itself ("the dog chases the cat." forever), because nothing changes from one sentence to the next. Drawing words by weighted chance instead gives variety. Real chatbots face the same choice.
  • Draw a word is random, but random doesn't mean every word gets an equal chance. It works like a raffle in which each word holds tickets in proportion to its probability. After "the dog chases the", "cat" holds about 996 of every 1,000 tickets, so it wins about 996 draws in 1,000; the other words can win, but rarely do. That's why a draw usually matches the likeliest word: the model is often nearly certain. Tinyspeak only has real choices in two places, which animal starts a sentence (dog 58%, cat 42%) and which action follows it, so try drawing right after "the" or after "the cat", several times each. If every word had an equal 1-in-8 chance instead, the model's predictions would be thrown away and you'd get gibberish like "the sleeps . dog chases chases".
  • Type nonsense like "dog dog dog" or "sleeps sleeps". The equation still answers, because it's a formula and formulas always produce a number. What its answers mean on inputs it never saw is a question for chapter 14.
  • In the number editor, move sees away from chases by changing its embedding. Or change a single number in \(\WQ\) and watch who-chases-whom fall apart.

Where the 44 numbers came from

In chapter 1, data chose two numbers for a line. Here, data chose 44 numbers for a formula. The data is a text written in a made-up language, Tinyspeak, with three rules:

  1. Every sentence starts with the and an animal, cat or dog, equally often.
  2. Then comes an action. Cats are lazy and dogs are busy: a cat sleeps 35% of the time, sees 25%, runs 20% and chases 20%; a dog chases 35%, runs 25%, sees 20% and sleeps 20%.
  3. runs and sleeps end the sentence. chases and sees are followed by the and the other animal, and then the sentence ends.

These rules produced a training text of 39,845 sentences. The model was never shown the rules, only the text. Here's how it starts:

Measuring how wrong a model is

For a line, the error was the area of the squares. For a language model, the standard measure is surprise. At every position in the text, ask the model how likely it thought the word that actually came next was, and score that as \(-\log P\) (minus the logarithm from chapter 2, so that it comes out positive). A model that gave the right word probability 1 has surprise 0. Probability 0.5 gives 0.69, and 0.01 gives 4.6. The loss \(L\) is the average surprise over all 179,416 predictions in the text.

Then training is the same move as least squares, with \(\theta\) standing for all 44 numbers and \(D\) for the data:

\[ \theta^{*} \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; L(\theta;\, D) \]

"\(\arg\min\)" means "the choice of \(\theta\) that makes \(L\) smallest". The star marks the winner.

How do you find it without trying every possible list of 44 numbers? Start with random numbers. For each of the 44, ask: if I nudged this number up a tiny bit, would the surprise go up or down? Move every number a small step in the helpful direction. Repeat a few thousand times. (This page computes all 44 answers at once using the chain rule from calculus, a method called backpropagation, but the answer is the same as actually nudging each number. The demo below also adds a standard refinement called Adam. Chapter 12 explains both, and shows why the refinement matters.)

Notice that the loss can't reach zero. The text itself flips a coin over which animal starts each sentence and rolls dice over the verb, and no model can predict a coin flip. The best possible loss for this text is 0.455. A model can get very close to that floor, but never below it.

Now try a few different random starts. Most of them never reach the dashed line. A common outcome is a loss of around 0.50 to 0.53: a model that has learned what kind of word comes next but treats "who gets chased" as close to a coin flip. That's the one rule that needs attention to look back at the subject. Other starts stall higher still, with even the grammar half learned. Training finds a set of numbers by walking downhill, and it can settle in a valley that isn't the deepest one. In twelve random starts tried while building this page, exactly one reached the bottom, and the model used throughout this page grew from that same start. (It was trained by literally nudging each number, while this demo uses the faster calculus method, so the two runs take slightly different paths. If you train start 1 here and compare, about half the numbers differ, by at most 0.3. Same behavior, slightly different equation.)

12

How training works, step by step

Chapter 11 described training in one sentence: start with random numbers, then keep nudging every number a little in whichever direction lowers the surprise. This chapter takes that sentence apart. First on the straight line from chapter 1, where the whole search fits in one picture, and then on the language model, with its real numbers.

Walking downhill on a line

In chapter 1, "Find the best line" jumped straight to the answer. It could, because for straight lines there's a formula that gives the best \(a\) and \(b\) directly. For almost every other model, including every language model, no such formula exists. The best numbers have to be searched for.

To make the search easy to picture, label each line by two numbers that can be drawn as a map: \(b\), how much it climbs per year (as in chapter 1), and \(c\), the income it predicts for someone with 14 years of education, the middle of the data. These are the same lines as before, just labeled differently (\(a = c - 14b\)). Every point on the map below is one possible line. The rings work like contour lines on a hiking map: each ring joins lines with the same total squared error (the number on it), and the error drops ring by ring toward the center. The best line sits at the bottom of that bowl, marked with a green circle.

Each step does the same two things for each number:

  1. Measure the slope. Nudge the number up a tiny bit and see how much the error changes, per unit of nudge. A big positive slope means "raising this number makes things much worse"; a negative slope means raising it helps.
  2. Step against the slope. Move the number by the slope times a small, fixed amount called the step size (or learning rate), in the downhill direction.

In symbols, for any adjustable number \(\theta\) and a step size \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta):

\[ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \eta \times \text{slope} \]

The arrow means "replace with". Steep places give big steps and gentle places give small ones, so the walk slows down by itself as it nears the bottom, where the ground is flat.

Now try the step-size slider. Small steps crawl. Larger steps get there faster, until they start overshooting: the path zigzags from one side of the valley to the other. Past about 0.025, every step lands higher up the far side than it started, and the error explodes. Notice also that the path doesn't head straight for the bottom. The bowl is much steeper in \(b\) than in \(c\), so \(b\) settles almost at once while \(c\) creeps the rest of the way. Choosing step sizes, and coping with valleys that are steep one way and shallow another, are two of the main practical problems in training.

One step for the language model, with real numbers

The language model works the same way, with 44 numbers instead of 2. A 44-dimensional bowl can't be drawn, but every step can still be computed and shown. Below, the model is at random start 1 from chapter 11, before any training. Choose a situation from the training text, measure a slope by nudging, then take a step.

Take a step on "cat chases the → dog" alone and the probability of dog jumps, but the loss over the whole text gets slightly worse. The step pushed the numbers to suit one prediction at the expense of others. That's why a real step uses the average slope over the training text. Every prediction pulls on the 44 numbers in its own direction, and the step follows the combined pull. Try "Take one step on the whole text" a few times and watch the overall loss fall.

The shortcut: predicted minus actual

Measuring slopes by nudging costs one extra run of the whole model for every number: 44 extra runs per step here, and billions for a large model. That would make training large models impossible. The method actually used, backpropagation, gets every slope for roughly the cost of one extra run, by working backwards through the equation and reusing work along the way.

It starts with the eight scores from chapter 8, the ones softmax turns into probabilities. For each word, ask the same question as before: if this word's score were nudged up a tiny bit, how much would the surprise change? That slope is called the word's error signal (it's also called the gradient). A positive error signal means raising the score would make the prediction worse, so the step lowers it. A negative error signal means raising it would help, so the step raises it.

Here is the remarkably simple fact. Writing \(t\) for any one of the eight words:

\[ \text{error signal for word } t \;=\; \underbrace{p_t}_{\text{probability the model gave it}} \;-\; \underbrace{y_t}_{1 \text{ if it came next, else } 0} \]

For example, if the model gave "dog" a probability of 0.16 and "dog" really came next, dog's error signal is \(0.16 - 1 = -0.84\): strongly negative, so dog's score goes up. If it gave "chases" 0.16 and chases didn't come next, the error signal for chases is \(0.16 - 0 = +0.16\), so its score goes down a little.

So the downhill step raises the score of the word that actually came next, in proportion to how badly it was under-predicted, and lowers every other word's score in proportion to the probability it wrongly took. Everything else in training follows from passing these error signals backwards through the equation, one step at a time, in the reverse of the order the prediction was computed. At each stage the rule comes from the chain rule of calculus, and the pattern is always the same: the slope for a matrix's number is what flowed into it times the error signal that came back to it.

Show the math for every backward step

Write \(\delta\) (delta) for "the error signal for". Going backwards from the surprise \(-\log p_{\text{target}}\), with \(y\) the actual next word written as eight 0s and a 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta(\text{scores}) &= p - y \\ \text{slope}(\WO) &= r^{\T}\,\delta(\text{scores}), \qquad \delta(r) = \delta(\text{scores})\,\WO^{\T} \\ \delta(\zv) &= \delta(r), \qquad \delta(\xn) \mathrel{+}= \delta(r) \\ \delta(\vv_j) &= \av_j\,\delta(\zv), \qquad \delta(\av_j) = \vv_j \cdot \delta(\zv) \\ \delta(s_j) &= \av_j\Big(\delta(\av_j) - \textstyle\sum_i \av_i\,\delta(\av_i)\Big) \\ \delta(\qv) &= \textstyle\sum_j \delta(s_j)\,\kv_j / \sqrt{2}, \qquad \delta(\kv_j) = \delta(s_j)\,\qv / \sqrt{2} \\ \text{slope}(\WV) &= \textstyle\sum_j x_j^{\T}\,\delta(\vv_j), \quad \text{slope}(\WK) = \textstyle\sum_j x_j^{\T}\,\delta(\kv_j), \quad \text{slope}(\WQ) = \xn^{\T}\,\delta(\qv) \\ \delta(x_j) &\mathrel{+}= \delta(\vv_j)\,\WV^{\T} + \delta(\kv_j)\,\WK^{\T}, \qquad \delta(\xn) \mathrel{+}= \delta(\qv)\,\WQ^{\T} \\ \text{slope}(\Ev\text{ row for word } w) &= \text{sum of } \delta(x_j) \text{ over the positions where } w \text{ appears} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(s_j\) are the scaled attention scores. "\(\mathrel{+}=\)" means "add to what's already there", because some numbers are used along several paths and collect an error signal from each. The slope over the whole text is the average of these over every prediction in it. The code that runs this page does exactly these steps (it's in js/model.js).

What real training adds

Plain downhill steps are the heart of it, but on their own they often stall. Here are 3,000 plain steps from random start 1, side by side with the method the demo in chapter 11 actually uses:

  • Adam. The demo in chapter 11 doesn't step by exactly step size × slope. It uses Adam, a standard refinement that does two things. It keeps a running average of each number's recent slopes, so steps carry some momentum through flat or bumpy stretches. And it scales each number's step by how large that number's slopes have typically been, so every number moves at a sensible pace even when the valley is steep one way and shallow another, like the line's bowl. With plain steps, the same start stalls well above the best possible loss; with Adam it reaches the bottom. Almost all large models are trained with Adam or a close relative.
  • Batches. Our demos average the slope over the whole training text at every step. That's affordable here, because the text contains only 31 different situations. Real training texts have trillions of words, so each step instead averages over a random handful of examples, called a batch. The result is a noisy estimate of the true slope, but it's far cheaper, and the noise averages out over many steps.
  • Scale. A large model takes hundreds of thousands of steps, each one adjusting billions of numbers at once, spread across thousands of chips running for weeks. The rule in each step is the one on this page.

Notice what that means. The rule that produced our 44 numbers is short, fully specified and completely understood: keep stepping downhill on surprise. Everything about the numbers follows from that rule, the training text and the random start. Yet knowing the rule doesn't tell you what the resulting equation does, any more than knowing how evolution works tells you how a particular animal works. That gap is the subject of chapter 14.

From 44 numbers to billions

Real language models are built from the same pieces, multiplied enormously:

Every addition is still made of multiplication, addition, exponentials and a few other ordinary functions. So the summary from chapter 9 holds at any size:

\[ P(\text{next token} \mid \text{previous tokens}) \;=\; F_{\theta^*}(\text{previous tokens}) \]

Read \(F_{\theta^*}\) as "the function \(F\), with the trained numbers \(\theta^*\) plugged in".

A real model is one function. In principle you could write it out the way chapter 9 did. In practice: our 44-number model already needs about 12,000 characters for one word's probability. A model with 70 billion numbers, printed at five characters per number, would fill something like 350,000 thick books before writing a single plus sign, and in the fully expanded formula each of those numbers would appear many times over.

The other thing that grows is the gap between the formula and our understanding of it. That's the subject of the last chapter.

14

Is the equation the best description there is?

We now have the complete equation of a language model. Do we understand it?

Two kinds of knowing

There's a difference between having a complete specification of a function and having an intelligible theory of what the function is doing. The equation in chapter 9 is the first kind. It answers every question of the form "what does the model output for this input?" exactly. On its own it doesn't answer questions like: does the model have something like the concept "animal"? Does it follow rules? Why does it handle new cases the way it does? Is there a much shorter description that captures what it does?

For our tiny model, we can actually check.

Our model has a short theory, on familiar ground

Tinyspeak's three rules predict what should follow every phrase that can occur in the language. There are 31 such phrases of up to three words. Compare the rules with the equation on each one:

They agree everywhere. For this model, on this ground, a few sentences of English describe what 44 numbers do. And we can say more than that by reading the numbers. In the embeddings, chases and sees are identical, so when they appear in the input the model can't tell them apart: to it they're the same kind of word. (They do get different arrows in \(\WO\), because cats and dogs use them at different rates.) In the attention weights, "the" looks back toward the subject (entirely after "cat chases the", partly after "dog chases the"), and an animal looks back for a verb or a period to know whether it's the subject or the object. Those are mechanistic explanations, the kind interpretability research tries to find in real models: a story about how the numbers produce the behavior.

But the equation answers questions the rules don't

With 8 words and a window of 3, there are 8 + 64 + 512 = 584 possible inputs. Only 31 of them can occur in Tinyspeak. The other 553 can't occur in Tinyspeak at all, so the rules were never meant to cover them. Sometimes you can stretch a rule to make a guess (after "sleeps", a sentence ends), and the equation doesn't always follow it. For many phrases, like "dog dog dog", no rule applies at all. The equation answers every one anyway:

So which is the real description of what this model does? The three rules are short and intelligible, and they describe the model's behavior on 31 inputs out of 584. The equation describes all 584, since it is the model, and nobody would call it intelligible at a glance. The only complete descriptions are the equation itself or a 584-row table of its answers.

Even the numbers aren't unique

Here's a stranger fact. Double every number in \(\WQ\) and halve every number in \(\WK\). Every query gets twice as long and every key half as long, so every dot product, and therefore every prediction, stays exactly the same. So "the" equation of a trained model is really one member of a large family of equations with identical behavior. Whatever understanding the model consists of, it can't be a fact about any particular number taken alone.

A ladder of descriptions

Put the options in order from most complete to most intelligible:

DescriptionFor this page's modelFor a large model
Lookup table: every input and its output584 rows of 8 probabilitiesImpossible to write: with 100,000 tokens and a long window, the number of possible inputs dwarfs the number of atoms in the universe many times over

Each step down is shorter and easier to understand, and may leave things out. The deep question is about the bottom of the ladder: could there be a language model for which the giant equation is essentially the best description possible, where no substantially simpler set of higher-level rules captures what it does?

Three possible answers

A compact theory exists

Physics has a famous precedent. A container of gas holds around \(10^{23}\) molecules, each moving in its own way, and yet pressure, volume and temperature obey a law you can write on a napkin. The details average out into a few quantities that matter. If language models are like this, interpretability's job is to find their equivalent of temperature: a handful of high-level variables that explain most of the behavior.

Many local explanations, no global one

Researchers have found real mechanisms inside real models. One example is "induction heads", attention heads that notice a pattern earlier in the text and continue it (Olsson and colleagues, 2022). Another is a circuit in the small model GPT-2 that works out which name in a sentence should come next in phrases like "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to…" (Wang and colleagues, 2022). Each explains one slice of behavior. It's an open question whether such slices eventually add up to a theory of the whole model, or whether understanding stays piecemeal: genuine, but local.

The equation is essentially the shortest faithful description

A basic result from the theory of information says that most long lists of numbers have no description much shorter than the list itself. There simply aren't enough short descriptions to go around. A trained network isn't a random list. It was fitted to highly structured data, and structure in the data should leave structure in the numbers. But that structure could be spread thinly across many numbers at once (researchers have shown small models storing more features than they have dimensions, overlapping them in "superposition"; Elhage and colleagues, 2022). If so, any short set of rules would be a lossy summary: true for typical cases, silent or wrong at the edges, much like Tinyspeak's rules on our strange inputs.

Why the question is hard to settle

Suppose no one finds a simple theory of a large model. That wouldn't prove there isn't one. The length of the shortest possible description of a thing (its Kolmogorov complexity) is provably uncomputable: there is no general method that can confirm a description is the shortest. And some processes may be computationally irreducible, in Stephen Wolfram's phrase: there's no shortcut to knowing what they'll do other than running them step by step.

Biology offers a sobering example. The tiny worm C. elegans has 302 neurons, and its complete wiring diagram was published in 1986. Decades later, scientists are still far from deriving its behavior from that diagram. A complete specification of a system and an explanation of it are different achievements, which is the same gap our 44-number equation already shows on a small scale.

What would follow if the equation were the best description?

  • Prediction. To know what the model will do on a genuinely new input, you might have to run it. The formula would be its own shortest explanation.
  • Trust. Assurances about behavior would have to come from testing, measurement, limits on what the system can do, and monitoring, rather than from reading off a set of rules.
  • Understanding. Explanation might shift from the fitted function to the process that produced it: the data and the fitting. Biology explains organisms through evolution without deriving each one, and we understand Tinyspeak's model best through Tinyspeak.
  • Partial pictures. A description can be good enough for one purpose without being complete. The philosopher Daniel Dennett called the patterns that make such descriptions useful "real patterns". The three rules are a real pattern in our model even though they don't cover 553 of its 584 possible inputs.

Questions to take with you

  • If two different lists of numbers produce exactly the same behavior, which one is "the" model, and what does understanding it consist of?
  • Is a description that's right on every input you'll ever see an explanation, or just a summary? Does it matter which inputs are left out?
  • As models grow, does a short theory become harder to find, or does it stop existing? Could we tell the difference?
  • Would we recognize the right high-level description if we found it, or might it use concepts we don't yet have?

In chapter 1, the fitted line had two numbers and each meant something you could say in words. Our language model has 44 numbers, and with effort we could still read meaning from them. With billions, the open question is whether meaning lives in the numbers at a level we can read, or only in the whole.
